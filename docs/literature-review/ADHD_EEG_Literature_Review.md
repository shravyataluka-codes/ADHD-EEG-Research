# Critical Literature Review for ADHD EEG Classification on the 144-Child Corpus

**Evidence cutoff: October 23, 2026.** This review synthesizes the completed 82-paper search and prioritizes primary studies. Full-text evidence was examined for the core 144-child studies by Vahid et al., Wang et al., and He et al.; for recent evaluation studies by García-Ponsoda et al., Attallah, Amini et al., Kim et al., Li et al., Geethapriya and Pattabiraman, Aggul, Gomez-Rivera et al., Sanchis et al., and Liang et al.; and for methodological studies by Brookshire et al. and Del Pup et al. Other 2025–2026 claims are labeled abstract/metadata-only. Search relevance is not treated as proof of dataset identity or methodological quality.

## Executive assessment

The target cohort is identifiable: it is the Dresden event-related EEG cohort described by Vahid et al., with 144 children—44 healthy controls (HC), 52 ADD, and 48 ADHD—and 33,902 retained trials. Wang et al. and He et al. both explicitly report using this same cohort; He et al. says so directly in its full text. The data are linked from the papers to the [OSF deposit](https://osf.io/6594x/). This corpus is distinct from the often-used Nasrabadi IEEE DataPort dataset, which has 121 children, 61 ADHD and 60 controls, a visual-counting task, 19 channels, and no ADD class. Results on those two datasets are not directly comparable without accounting for task, labels, acquisition, preprocessing, and participant splits \[Vah19, Wan22, He23, Agg26, Gee26\].

A new Transformer, an explainability module, or subject-independent evaluation alone is not a defensible novelty claim. Transformer-based classification of this exact cohort was published by He et al. in 2023; participant-held-out evaluation and saliency-based interpretation were already present in Vahid et al. in 2019, and Wang et al. reported both trial-split and leave-one-child-out results in 2022 \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\]. More recent papers also combine Transformers, interpretation, and participant-wise evaluation on other pediatric ADHD EEG datasets \[Gom26, Pas26, Sal25\].

The clearest unresolved issue is not which architecture wins, but whether claimed three-class performance on the 144-child cohort survives a fully documented participant-disjoint evaluation with data-dependent steps confined to training folds and predictions summarized at the child level. Direct evidence is mixed: Wang et al. report 98.23% under trial-based five-fold evaluation but 39.59% mean accuracy under leave-one-subject-out evaluation; Vahid et al. likewise report poor average three-class performance across the full cohort, with much higher binary results only in a selected above-chance subset \[Vah19, Wan22\]. He et al.’s full text confirms the 144-child data but describes a trial-level split and does not establish participant-exclusive test data \[He23\].

A defensible project could therefore be a rigorous replication and uncertainty audit of the 144-child corpus—not a claim that a particular architecture or validation unit is new. Its value would depend on exact reproduction of the cohort and trial construction, a locked subject-level protocol, clear separation of primary three-class and secondary binary questions, and open release of code, splits, and per-child predictions. Even that contribution is conditional: Vahid and Wang already attempted subject-held-out evaluation, so the novelty would be in resolving reproducibility and reporting conflicts, not in being the first to hold out participants \[Vah19, Wan22\].

| Other pediatric cohorts | For example, Kim et al. analyzed 168 children from three clinical sites, 107 ADHD and 61 neurotypical controls; Han et al. analyzed 61 children, 31 ADHD and 30 controls \[Kim25b, Han24\]. | Kim et al. used multi-device, eyes-closed resting EEG; Han et al. used a continuous visual-attention task \[Kim25b, Han24\]. | LOSO is reported by Kim et al., but the published confusion matrix is based on 3,494 EEG segments rather than 168 child-level decisions. Han et al. report 10-fold CV without enough detail to verify participant grouping \[Kim25b, Han24\]. | Useful methodological comparators, not replications of the 144-child cohort. |

The 144-child cohort’s clinical labels are reported as professionally established using clinical indicators, home/school interviews, questionnaires, IQ and attention testing. Wang et al. additionally describe clinical diagnoses “such as ICD-10.” The papers do not provide a complete participant-level diagnostic record or enough detail to independently audit label assignment \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\].

There is also a representation discrepancy worth resolving before reimplementation. Vahid et al. describe 56-channel single-trial epochs from −200 to 3,000 ms at 500 Hz, then CSD transformation. Wang and He describe 1.5-second trials at 256 Hz, with 385 time points. At 256 Hz, 1.5 seconds corresponds to 384 intervals, while the reported tensor has 385 points; the papers do not explain the extra point or map the 1.5-second tensors back to Vahid’s epoch boundaries. Wang and He also do not fully reproduce Vahid’s CSD and artifact pipeline descriptions \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\].

The He23 result requires careful wording: the full text verifies both the dataset identity and the trial-level evaluation description. It does not verify that children were separated between train, validation, and test. The appropriate conclusion is “participant independence is undocumented,” not “leakage was proven.” Trial splitting makes participant overlap possible, and the direct Wang comparison shows that performance can change sharply when the split unit changes \[Wan22, He23\].

## Development of methods and evaluation

| Period | Method shift | What the evidence shows |
|:---|:---|:---|
| 2019 | EEGNet applied to event-related EEG in the Dresden cohort \[Vah19\]. | Participant-held-out evaluation existed early. Across the full cohort, three-class performance was low and heterogeneous; subtype discrimination was weak. Selected-subset binary accuracies should not be read as performance in all children \[Vah19\]. |
| 2020–2022 | Classical feature models and CNNs on the separate Nasrabadi binary cohort; CNN-LSTM on the Dresden three-class cohort \[Maf22, Ali23, Wan22\]. | Very high segment-level scores appear in several papers, but split units are often unclear. Wang’s own random-trial versus LOOCV results show that model metrics on the exact cohort are not comparable across split protocols \[Wan22\]. |
| 2023 | EEG-Transformer on the Dresden cohort; explainable boosted-tree and CNN approaches also appear on ADHD EEG \[He23, Kha23, Loh23\]. | Transformer and explainability are already part of the literature. He23’s high result is trial-level by the described protocol; ADHD/CD-NET and other 2023 methods use additional datasets and do not establish participant-wise transfer to the 144-child cohort \[He23, Kha23, Loh23\]. |
| 2024 | Preprocessing and feature-selection studies, attention models, and explicit discussion of EEG data leakage \[Gar24, Att24, Han24, Bro24, Kin24\]. | Preprocessing choices can change scores; high scores from random folds are hard to interpret if the split unit and selection nesting are unclear. Brookshire et al. directly tested leakage in Alzheimer’s and seizure EEG, not ADHD, so it supports the mechanism but is not direct proof about this cohort \[Gar24, Att24, Bro24\]. |
| 2025 | More attention, graph, hybrid and Transformer models; some studies report nested or subject-wise protocols \[Hu25, Ami25, Kim25b, Li25, Sal25\]. | Architecture and explainability claims proliferate. Full-text audits reveal that a subject-wise label does not alone guarantee unbiased estimates: test-fold-informed augmentation choice, segment-level outcome aggregation, or unreported fold assignment remain concerns \[Ami25, Kim25b, Li25\]. |
| 2026 | Stronger emphasis on group splits, participant-level aggregation, nested validation, leakage audits, and cross-cohort transfer \[Gee26, Agg26, Gom26, San26b, Lia26, Roh26\]. | Subject-wise evaluation is not a novel contribution by itself. The better-designed studies often report materially lower performance than random-window scores, and Transformer models are not consistently superior to simpler baselines \[Gee26, Agg26, Gom26, San26b\]. |

## Leakage, preprocessing, metrics, and reproducibility

Participant-disjoint splitting is necessary when the intended claim is performance on children absent from model training. All epochs from a child should stay together across train, validation, and test. The general mechanism is supported by Brookshire et al.’s direct Alzheimer’s and seizure EEG experiments, but those experiments did not use ADHD; ADHD-specific evidence comes from later studies on the separate Nasrabadi cohort \[Bro24, Gee26, Agg26\]. Wang’s same-corpus LOOCV result and Vahid’s earlier held-out-child results make the concern directly relevant to the 144-child cohort \[Vah19, Wan22\].

A subject-grouped outer split is not enough if model choices are made after inspecting outer test results. Scaling, channel/feature selection, augmentation selection, thresholds, and hyperparameters must be fixed in advance or selected only within training data. Amini et al.’s report of “optimal” augmentation based on outer-test performance, Attallah’s unclear placement of feature selection relative to CV, and the fold-wide configuration selection described by Gomez-Rivera et al. show why the full pipeline—not just the split function—must be audited \[Ami25, Att24, Gom26\]. The 2024 and 2025 partitioning studies also show that validation reuse and nested versus non-nested designs can alter EEG estimates; they do not establish an ADHD-specific numerical correction \[Bro24, Pup25\].

For the 144-child corpus, the child is the independent unit. Subject counts (44 HC, 52 ADD, 48 ADHD) are fairly close, but retained trial counts differ by class (10,129, 13,031, and 10,742); a 33,902-trial test set does not provide 33,902 independent diagnostic cases, and trial-level metrics can weight children unevenly. If trial predictions are combined into a child-level decision, fix the aggregation rule before testing and give each child equal weight in primary metrics. Report per-class sensitivity/recall, specificity, macro-F1, balanced accuracy, confusion matrix, and participant-level confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals. Report trial-level metrics only as secondary signal-level analyses \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\].

Preprocessing comparisons are also not directly transferable across cohorts. García-Ponsoda et al. report performance changes across filtering, ASR, and ICA on Nasrabadi data; Sanchis et al. report ASR plus FASTER and LOSO on the same separate cohort. These studies motivate a preprocessing audit but do not establish which pipeline is best for the Dresden task \[Gar24, San26b\]. If the available 144-child files are already ICA-cleaned, CSD-transformed, or reduced to 1.5-second trials, the original filter, reference, artifact criteria, and epoch construction may not be recoverable. Do not claim to compare preprocessing choices without access to the needed raw signals and metadata \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\].

Reproducibility remains uneven. Wang et al. report an OSF data source and a code repository; Vahid et al. link the data and EEGNet implementation but do not provide a full modern pipeline card; He et al. provide a data link but no study-specific code repository. The newer papers vary from open code and participant splits to request-only code and incomplete reporting \[Vah19, Wan22, He23, Att24, Gee26, Agg26, Gom26, San26b\]. The 2026 DSAEN abstract also claims a controlled reproduction of prior models on the 121-child cohort, but its split-by-split details and code need full-text verification before treating it as a settled benchmark \[Das26\].

## Claims that still need verification before a novelty statement

- Whether SCANet’s 99.78% three-class result uses the Vahid 144-child corpus, and whether its folds are grouped by participant \[Hu25\].
- Whether GAFNet genuinely uses both the Vahid and Nasrabadi datasets, how it defines ADD versus ADHD-C, and whether its “cross-dataset” test set is independent after domain-adaptation and model selection \[Sag26\].
- Whether He23’s reported 6,000-trial test samples are participant-overlapping across training/validation/test; the full text does not document grouping, so this remains unknown, not a proven leak \[He23\].
- The exact relationship between Vahid’s 56-channel, 500-Hz, 3.2-second epochs and the 256-Hz, 385-point inputs reported by Wang and He; resolve whether this is a cropped/reprocessed derivative or a reporting inconsistency \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\].
- He23’s 95.85% abstract accuracy versus 95.58% ± 0.52% full-text comparison, meaning of the ± term, conflicting parameter counts, 144-versus-300 sample-size statement, and unspecified statistical comparison details \[He23\].
- San26b’s 88.42% uploaded full-text result versus 87.6% indexed abstract result and the version status of the uploaded preprint versus the journal record \[San26b\].
- For any candidate 144-child reanalysis: availability of participant identifiers, exact per-child trial counts, original clinical diagnostic documentation, raw versus preprocessed signal files, reference/filter/ICA/CSD settings, and whether the task and time window are the same across records \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\].
- For all recent abstract-only papers: dataset identity, participant-level aggregation, nested tuning, class-wise metrics, uncertainty estimates, code availability, and whether reported explanations were tested for stability rather than shown as illustrative maps \[Hu25, Sal25, Pas26, Sag26, Zav26, Dhi26, Ham26, Pre26\].

## Research decision

The evidence does not support choosing a Transformer or claiming novelty for explainability or subject-wise evaluation alone. The strongest next research question is conditional: **Can the published high trial-level three-class performance on the Dresden 144-child cohort be reproduced as reliable child-level discrimination when the entire pipeline is locked and all data-dependent choices are nested within participant-disjoint folds?** This question is important because the same cohort has both near-ceiling trial-split results and low held-out-child results, and because the 2023 Transformer paper’s full text does not establish participant-independent testing \[Vah19, Wan22, He23\].

Before implementation, lock the target label contrast, exact trial representation, subject-to-fold assignments, child-level aggregation rule, primary metrics, and selection/tuning plan. Treat the three-class problem as primary only if the clinical meaning and provenance of ADD versus ADHD labels are preserved; predefine binary contrasts as secondary analyses rather than replacing the three-class task after seeing results. Include architecture comparisons only after this specification is fixed. This review does not select a final model architecture.
