# Reproducible ADHD/ADD classification on the Dresden EEG cohort: evidence and research gaps

## Executive assessment

A reproducibility-focused three-class study on the Dresden 144-child cohort is **conditionally defensible**, but not as a first participant-independent evaluation or as a new architecture paper. Vahid et al. (2019) already report leave-one-subject-out evaluation of EEGNet; Wang et al. (2022) explicitly report both random-trial five-fold validation and held-out-subject validation, with radically different results. The most defensible remaining contribution is an auditable, independently reproducible reanalysis that resolves the processed-data/metadata/label mapping and then compares prespecified, subject-disjoint three-class baselines with participant-level uncertainty. That contribution depends on first recovering a valid participant-to-trial map and the exact label/channel conventions; if those cannot be established, the dataset cannot support a reliable evaluation. [^1][^2]

A new broad claim that “ADHD EEG literature ignores subject-level validation” is already contradicted by Vahid, Wang, and multiple 2026 studies on the separate 121-child Nasrabadi cohort. The latter provide strong counterevidence to novelty based only on leakage-aware evaluation, but do not resolve the three-class Dresden problem because they use a binary task and another acquisition cohort. [^1][^2][^3][^4]

## A. Additional relevant literature beyond the initial shortlist

The most directly relevant additional work located is **“Window leakage in clinical EEG machine learning: matched subject-independent evaluation across ADHD and Parkinson cohorts” (2026)**. It uses the 121-child ADHD/control cohort, not Dresden, and compares random-window with participant-disjoint splits; random-window balanced accuracy of 95.59% for ADHD fell to 60.98% with participants held out. It also aggregates predictions at participant level and reports wide participant-bootstrap intervals despite many windows. This supports the importance of participant-level inference, but it does not test the Dresden three-class task. [^4]

A second directly relevant 2026 study, **“Subject-independent classification of ADHD from pediatric EEG”**, also uses the 121-child Nasrabadi dataset: 61 ADHD and 60 controls, 19 channels, 128 Hz, visual-attention recordings. It compared segment-wise and subject-wise validation, reporting a 24.4-point SVM accuracy drop (90.0% to 65.6%) and 72.7% subject-wise accuracy for its compact CNN. This is not independent evidence for Dresden performance, but it demonstrates that leakage-aware evaluation and rigorous nested training-fold handling have already been explored for a different pediatric dataset. Its claim that deterministic fold assignments and scripts are available only on reasonable request also illustrates a reproducibility limitation. [^3]

The 2026 **DSAEN** paper likewise reports a replication on 121 children (61 ADHD, 60 controls), with epoch-wise-to-subject-wise changes in published-model performance, and proposes a new network. It is relevant as evidence that the evaluation-leakage problem remains actively studied; it is not a Dresden three-class replication, and its abstract-level results should not be transferred to Dresden. [^5]

The initial shortlist’s **SCANet** is a 2025 publication reporting a three-class public dataset result and a separate private binary dataset result. Its abstract reports 99.78% accuracy on the public three-class data, but the retrieved evidence does not establish the exact cohort, split unit, subject-disjointness, preprocessing fit boundaries, or full-text details. The abstract’s mention of “public three-class” is insufficient to certify that the evaluation is a clean participant-exclusive Dresden test. Its very high reported metric should therefore be treated as an author-reported abstract claim, not an independently verified generalization result. [^6]

The proposed **GAFNet (2026)** search record claims to combine the Nasrabadi 121-participant and Vahid 144-participant benchmarks and reports binary and three-class results. However, this research pass did not verify a primary-paper full text or a reliable publication record for the exact title, nor establish its evaluation unit or reproduce its cohort-specific results. It should remain **unverified and excluded from confirmed evidence** pending a DOI/publisher record, original manuscript, code/data provenance, and protocol details. A search-record abstract is not enough to support performance or novelty claims. [^7]

Other studies identified in the broader search include numerous ADHD EEG classifiers that use the separate 121-child cohort, other local EEG cohorts, adult ADHD data, or conduct-disorder/ADHD tasks. They do not establish evidence about the Dresden data unless the paper explicitly identifies the Dresden cohort or its OSF source. For example, a 2026 channel-attention paper reports 19-channel recordings from 121 children, a binary ADHD/control task and sliding-window segments; it must not be conflated with the 144-child Dresden cohort. [^8]

## B. What is verified for studies using the exact Dresden cohort

### Vahid et al. (2019): participant-exclusive evaluation was already done

This original paper used the Dresden sample of 144 children: 44 controls, 52 ADD/inattentive subtype, and 48 ADHD/combined subtype. Diagnoses for 100 clinical participants were based on clinical assessment, and the paper describes the task as event-related EEG during interval timing. It recorded 60 electrodes at 500 Hz, removed P9/P10/P11/P12 for analysis, used 56 channels, and reports band-pass filtering (0.5–20 Hz), manual technical artifact review, ICA for pulse/eye artifacts, stimulus-locked epochs, baseline correction, automated artifact rejection, and current-source-density transformation. [^1]

The original evaluation is genuinely **participant-independent**: each child was held out in turn, with training participants separated from test participants and validation participants sampled from the training side. Across all participants, EEGNet’s mean single-trial three-class accuracy was 39% (SD 30%) and individual performance ranged from 1% to 99%. The paper then reports higher discrimination analyses restricted to a subset selected for above-chance individual classification; that conditional subset result must not be presented as the whole-cohort estimate. The three-class result in that subset was 69% (SD 28%), while distinguishing ADD from ADHD remained near chance. [^1]

The study therefore already addresses the central question “does a model generalize to an unseen child?”—at least for EEGNet, the authors’ original preprocessing, and their reported protocol. Important limitations remain: individual held-out accuracies were highly variable, the highlighted 69% estimate conditions on selecting above-chance participants, and the authors themselves state that the data cannot establish diagnostic specificity and that reliable classification was possible only for a subset. [^1]

### Wang et al. (2022): two protocols, different estimands

Wang et al. explicitly identify the same OSF source and reproduce the same 144 children and 44/52/48 diagnostic counts. Their processed model input is described as 33,902 event-related trials, 56 channels, and 385 time points, downsampled to 256 Hz, for a stated 1.5-second trial. They state 0.5–20 Hz filtering and ICA/artifact preprocessing, followed by raw time-domain input to the model. [^2]

The headline **98.23%** accuracy is from five-fold random trial splitting: each fold selects 6,000 of the 33,902 trials as test data. Since multiple trials belong to each child, that is trial-independent but not participant-independent. The same paper separately reports leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, with each child held out, and a mean CNN-LSTM accuracy of 39.59% (SD 28.11%) across the 144 held-out children; some subjects exceeded 90% and others were below 10%. It reports 33.9–37.79% for three comparison CNN models. These results are not contradictory: they answer different test questions, and the high trial-wise result does not estimate performance on unseen children. [^2]

Wang et al. are unusually explicit in noting that trial-level splitting can place a participant’s trials in both training and test sets, while subject-level training has only 144 independent participant units. They characterize subject-level model performance as poor and highly variable. The study did not report a distinct locked external test cohort; its LOSO protocol is nevertheless a bona fide participant-disjoint evaluation. [^2]

### He et al. (2023): impressive metric, protocol unresolved from retrieved original-source evidence

The retrieved paper record reports EEG-Transformer average accuracy of 95.85% and AUC 0.9926, and compares the model with three CNNs. The accessible record in this research pass did not provide full text or enough dataset and protocol detail to verify cohort identity, class composition, split unit, leakage controls, or whether the reported number is trial- or subject-level. Thus the 95.85% is verified only as an abstract-reported metric; the dataset and participant-independent interpretation remain **unclear**, not confirmed. The initial project note’s characterization as trial-level cross-validation was not independently substantiated from the full text here. [^9]

## C. Trial-level versus participant-independent evidence

The 2019 and 2022 papers already establish the core distinction within the Dresden data. Vahid’s leave-one-subject-out setup places all trials from one child in the test fold and uses only other children for training. Wang’s five-fold protocol randomly samples trials from the full set, so it estimates prediction on additional trials from a population that may already be represented during training. Wang’s separate leave-one-child-out results directly quantify this discrepancy: 98.23% under trial splitting versus 39.59% (SD 28.11%) under participant holdout for CNN-LSTM. [^1][^2]

A crucial nuance: a subject-wise split is not synonymous with a high-performing or clinically useful model. Vahid reports a 39% whole-cohort mean (SD 30%) and selected-subset results; Wang reports strong subject-to-subject variation and low average held-out-child performance. Neither paper supports treating thousands of trials as thousands of independent children. [^1][^2]

The available evidence does **not** support a full audit of leakage in all recent Dresden papers. Wang describes validation-set early stopping and random training/validation trials under the five-fold protocol; because the same participant can contribute to both partitions, the validation process is also potentially participant-contaminated in that protocol. The paper says the LOSO validation participants are selected from training participants, which is the correct separation for the outer test child. The text retrieved does not establish whether all transformations or any hyperparameter decisions were refit independently inside each fold. Vahid’s paper reports preprocessing before model evaluation and per-fold subject separation, but does not sufficiently detail whether data-adaptive preprocessing was fit globally or only within the training fold. This is a reproducibility audit target, not proof that leakage occurred. [^2][^1]

The 121-child literature adds clear counterevidence to broad novelty claims. A 2026 study reports a 24.4-percentage-point change for SVM between segment-wise and participant-wise evaluation, and explicitly computes normalization on training-fold data; a separate 2026 cross-cohort leakage study reports participant-disjoint validation and participant-level aggregation. These address the general methodological concern, but neither resolves whether subject-wise three-class performance can be reliably reproduced on the Dresden data. [^3][^4]

## D. Research questions already addressed

1. **Can an ADHD EEG model be evaluated on unseen children in this cohort?** Yes. Vahid’s 2019 LOSO EEGNet analysis and Wang’s 2022 LOSO analyses do this; this is not an unaddressed gap. [^1][^2]
2. **Can trial-wise performance be much higher than subject-wise performance?** Yes. Wang directly compares the protocols on this cohort, and 2026 121-child work independently demonstrates the same general phenomenon on a different cohort. [^2][^3]
3. **Has anyone tested a three-class HC/ADD/ADHD task on Dresden?** Yes: Vahid (2019), Wang (2022), and SCANet’s abstract claims a public three-class evaluation, though SCANet’s exact data source/protocol are not verified here. [^1][^2][^6]
4. **Is architecture novelty alone adequate?** No. Wang compared CNN-LSTM with EEGNet, ShallowConvNet, and DeepConvNet, while SCANet’s abstract also claims comparison with prior architectures. Any new model would need a protocol-level contribution and fair same-split comparison, not just a different network name. [^2][^6]
5. **Has explainability been applied?** Yes. Vahid reports saliency maps and source-localization analyses; Wang visualizes convolution outputs and saliency maps; SCANet’s abstract reports Grad-CAM. Explainability alone is not a novelty claim. [^1][^2][^6]

## E. Specific unresolved questions

- **Does a reproducible implementation recover the published Dresden results under exactly specified partitions?** The sources provide descriptions and Wang links code, but this research pass did not retrieve or execute the code or verify an end-to-end recreation. The unresolved question is replication fidelity—particularly preprocessing, partition construction, seeds, stopping rules, and metric aggregation—not whether a subject-wise split has ever been attempted. [^2][^1]
- **What is the cohort-level estimate and uncertainty for participant-level three-class prediction?** Vahid’s 2019 estimate combines substantial participant variation with a secondary subset conditioned on above-chance subject classification; Wang reports LOSO accuracy variability but does not establish externally validated, calibrated participant-level performance. A prespecified out-of-fold aggregation and participant-bootstrap interval could clarify uncertainty, provided labels and participant mapping are verified. [^1][^2]
- **Are preprocessing and model-selection operations leakage-safe in a modern, reproducible rerun?** The published descriptions do not fully settle fold-wise fitting of all data-dependent transforms or selection decisions. A credible reanalysis should state, and if relevant demonstrate, that training-fold-only operations include normalization, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, artifact thresholds estimated from data, hyperparameter tuning, and early stopping; outer test participants must never drive those choices. This is a proposed audit criterion, not evidence that prior preprocessing was definitely leaked. [^1][^2]
- **Do the impressive 2023–2026 scores reflect this cohort and unseen-child performance?** He’s split method and cohort were not verifiable from retrieved full text; SCANet’s public cohort and split unit remain unspecified in the accessible abstract; GAFNet remains bibliographically unverified. Resolving these records could either strengthen or eliminate a gap claim. [^9][^6][^7]
- **Does three-class discrimination remain stable across alternative but defensible label definitions?** Vahid and Wang use 44 HC, 52 ADD, and 48 combined ADHD children; a reanalysis must verify how the provided coded labels map to these clinical groups before it claims to reproduce that task. The label-to-diagnosis mapping was not settled by the reviewed articles alone. [^1][^2]

## F. Dataset and methodological uncertainties affecting feasibility

1. **Clinical labels and mapping.** The papers define diagnostic groups and counts, but the project must link these definitions to the exact OSF files and coded labels. Verify class code meaning, participant IDs, exclusions, and whether one ID has multiple sessions or records before training. The studies’ group counts are not sufficient evidence that a downloaded file’s labels map correctly. [^1][^2]
2. **Participant-to-trial linkage.** Subject-exclusive splitting requires a trusted participant ID on every trial. Wang reports 33,902 trials with group-level counts, but the paper’s description alone does not prove the supplied processed arrays preserve a usable one-to-one ID-to-trial map. If that key is absent or ambiguous, a valid participant-independent evaluation is infeasible until recovered from source files or authors. [^2]
3. **385 samples versus duration/rate.** Wang reports 385 time points, a 256-Hz sampling rate, and a 1.5-second trial. A nominal 1.5 s at 256 samples/s is 384 sampling intervals; 385 stored points can be consistent with both endpoints included, but the time vector and epoch boundary convention need verification. Do not silently truncate, resample, or relabel the duration. [^2]
4. **56 signal channels versus 60 channel metadata entries.** Vahid reports 60 recorded electrodes, excludes P9/P10/P11/P12, and uses 56 channels; Wang likewise reports 56-channel input. If the currently inspected channel metadata has 60 entries, the extra four may correspond to removed sensors, but this cannot be assumed until exact names/order and array dimensions are reconciled. [^1][^2]
5. **Epoch-window/version mismatch.** Vahid describes stimulus-locked segments from 200 ms before to 3,000 ms after target onset, whereas Wang describes the processed model input as 1.5 seconds. These may be distinct preprocessing products or task windows; the underlying OSF files and processing scripts must establish whether the model inputs are genuinely comparable before exact score replication. [^1][^2]
6. **Preprocessing provenance and leakage.** The original paper reports filtering, artifact rejection, ICA, CSD, and epoching; Wang describes downsampled and denoised data. A faithful replication needs the precise order, software/parameters, reference/montage, whether preprocessing is per-recording or pooled, and which steps learn parameters across data. Preprocessing performed independently within a single child’s recording is different from transformations estimated using all participants. [^1][^2]
7. **Metric unit and class imbalance.** Trial-pooled scores weight participants with more retained trials more heavily. For unseen-child generalization, report participant-level predictions and both participant-level and trial-level metrics with the aggregation rule stated; use class-sensitive metrics and uncertainty based on children, not just trials. Recent work on the other cohort explicitly warns that segment counts do not determine inferential precision. [^1][^2][^4]

The observed 385/384 and 56/60 discrepancies are also explicitly listed as unresolved provenance checks in the supplied project brief; they should be treated as open audit items rather than resolved explanations. [^10]

## H. Recommendation and falsification conditions

**Recommendation: proceed only as a conditional reproducibility/benchmark study, not as a claim to be the first subject-independent Dresden analysis.** Vahid already used leave-one-subject-out EEGNet, and Wang reported both trial-wise and participant-wise validation for multiple CNN architectures. The value-add should be independent verification of the data artifacts and labels; a fully specified, frozen participant-disjoint evaluation; fold-contained fitting/model selection; per-child out-of-fold estimates with child-level uncertainty; code, environment, seeds, and data-processing manifests; and a clear reconciliation of previously published protocol/result discrepancies. [^1][^2]

The study’s primary endpoint should be defined at the **child**, not epoch, level: aggregate held-out trials into one prespecified prediction per child, report the three-class confusion matrix and class-wise performance, and quantify uncertainty by resampling children or reporting fold/child variation. A separate trial-level result can be included only as a secondary comparator and must be labeled as such. This distinction is supported by Wang’s reported protocol contrast and by later subject-independent EEG studies that emphasize participant-level aggregation and uncertainty. [^2][^4]

The proposed gap would be **invalidated or substantially narrowed** if (i) a verified full text for SCANet, He, or a confirmed GAFNet publication demonstrates a clean subject-disjoint three-class Dresden evaluation with reproducible participant-level results; (ii) a faithful reproduction of Vahid/Wang already provides the locked, fold-contained pipeline and uncertainty analysis proposed here; or (iii) OSF materials cannot establish participant IDs, diagnostic labels, and consistent channel/timepoint semantics. The first two conditions remove novelty; the third removes experimental feasibility. The sources currently available do not settle those tests, especially for He and SCANet because only abstract-level evidence was accessible, and for GAFNet because the paper record itself was not independently confirmed. [^9][^6][^7][^2][^10]

The defensible question is therefore: **Can the published Dresden three-class results be independently reproduced under a verified, locked participant-disjoint protocol, and what child-level performance and uncertainty remain after resolving the dataset’s label, participant, channel, and epoch provenance?** No architecture choice is justified until the cohort mapping and benchmark protocol pass that audit. [^1][^2]

## Evidence limits

The original full texts for Vahid (2019) and Wang (2022) were accessible and directly inspected. He (2023) and SCANet (2025) were assessed from abstract/record-level evidence only, so their dataset and split protocols remain unverified; the GAFNet record could not be corroborated against a primary manuscript or publication record in this pass. [^9][^6][^7] The literature search included multiple topic- and title-based queries across academic and web sources, but it was not a formal systematic review and cannot establish that no other Dresden analyses exist.
